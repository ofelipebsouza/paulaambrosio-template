I like to stand where the sofa will go before I decide how tall the sofa should be. A floor plan tells me what fits. Standing in the room tells me which chair will catch the morning light, where the horizon sits and how people will move from the living room to the terrace.

## Design the view from a seated position

A low sofa back or a pair of swivel chairs can make it easier to enjoy the glass and still talk to someone across the room. Leave a clear route to the terrace and test high pieces at eye level before buying. For television or reading, notice where the light falls at the hour you use the room most.

## Choose fabric for the light it will actually see

I take large samples to the window and look at them more than once. Light changes the way texture reads. Ask suppliers for their performance and cleaning information and whether the material is suited to direct sun or outdoor exposure. A sheer can soften glare while preserving the view. In a bedroom or media area, a separate privacy or blackout layer often works better than asking one fabric to do everything.

## Specify finishes for how they will be used

Think about which doors will be open to salt air, where wet feet land and how often a surface will be cleaned. Ask suppliers which hardware finishes are intended for coastal exposure, and request care instructions for stone, metal and wood before ordering. For a terrace, use furnishings and textiles intended for outdoor conditions and check the building’s rules before placing or securing anything outside.

In our [Delray Beach waterfront residence](https://www.paulaambrosio.com/projects/home-fl/), a palette of soft neutral textures makes natural light part of the room. The same idea can guide a condo by the ocean: leave room for the light and the view to change throughout the day.
