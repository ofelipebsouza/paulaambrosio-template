A built-in can look beautiful in a rendering and still be frustrating to use. A drawer can hit a door handle. An outlet can land behind a fixed panel. A run of cabinets can look uneven because the grain was never planned across the fronts. These are small decisions on paper, but they shape how a room looks and works every day.

## Start with what the room needs to do

At [HOME M in Miami](https://www.paulaambrosio.com/projects/home-m/), the home office brings a library and a work surface together in one built-in. The light wood ties the office to the rest of the residence, while the dark shelving gives the wall structure. Before choosing a finish for a piece like this, I want to know what needs to be within reach, what should be hidden, and how the person will actually use the room.

## Get the details right before fabrication

I look at three things in the shop drawings: where the grain and seams fall, what happens when every door and drawer is open, and how someone will reach outlets or lighting components after installation. I also like to review finish samples in the room itself. Wood, stone and hardware can look very different in daylight than they do under showroom lighting.

The priorities change from one South Florida home to the next. In a Miami Beach or Sunny Isles Beach condo, a media wall might need to hide equipment without making it difficult to service. In an Aventura apartment, an entry built-in might have to make room for shoes, bags and keys without narrowing the passage. In a Coral Gables or Boca Raton home, tall library shelving can do more for a room when it follows the proportions of the architecture.

Good craftsmanship shows up in the details you use without thinking: a door opens fully, the light falls where you need it, and the built-in feels like it was always meant to be there.
